# Working with an LLM
An LLM can use your historical training data to inform your training program. Fitdocs gives LLMs tools to explore and understand your training history. They can search the wiki’s pages, query the analytics database, and when necessary, read the underlying .fit files directly.

## Ask questions

With your wiki in front of it, a model can explain what a threshold is and where yours came from, walk through this morning’s intervals lap by lap, compare this month with the same month last year, and point out patterns that are hard to see in a spreadsheet. Ask it the questions you’d ask a knowledgeable friend, and when you don’t understand something, dig in and ask for a clearer explanation. Keep a skeptical mind, push back, and continue asking questions until you’re satisfied that the response is well founded.

LLMs can’t replace your judgement. After you’ve explored the possibilities, the decision is yours. The work runs in a loop: you train, fitdocs keeps the record, an LLM uses that record to inform your next steps, and you decide what to do next.

## A working rhythm
The sections below suggest ways to use fitdocs and an LLM to build a detailed training history.

### Daily
**After a workout** and before you look at your data, write down how you thought it went. Capture anything that seems relevant or useful to remember in the future. Document any niggles or discomfort, so you have an objective record and can catch an injury early.

Then, ask the LLM to explore the data and connect it back. How does your perception match up against the underlying metrics? If they differ, is there a clear explanation? Any red flags, or unexplained outlyers in the data? How does this workout fit into your recent training history? What’s your current fitness trajectory? Are there any signals that your existing training plan needs to be tweaked? 

Ask the LLM to summarize your recap into the notes section of your fitdocs page, where it’s saved to inform your future decisions.

### Mesocycle
**Once a week**, ask for a review: this week’s load against the four weeks before it, which pages carry warnings, and what your notes say about how you were feeling. A good review answers the questions you’re curious about and points out things you didn’t know to ask about. Be specific in the things you want to see, and ask the LLM to highlight things in the data that you’re not yet thinking about. Before you end the session, have the LLM summarize your insights onto the page.

### Block
**At the start of a block**, start by identifying your goals for this block. What do you want to achieve? When inevitably you can’t have it all, what is most important to you about your training? Is it reaching the highest levels of performance possible? Overcoming an injury cycle and maintaining consistency? Or establishing a healthy exercise routine that you enjoy? Talk your goals through with the LLM and ask whether they’re realistic, but insist on what you want. You’re the one who will live with your training block; take the time to plan a program you’re excited about.

Use your agent to research best practices and common wisdom around the type of training your most interested in. There are many different approaches to every discipline, and every one won’t be the right fit for you. Fitness is built over decades. When you find what works for you, build a core routine around it that you can hold for years. 

**At the end of a block**, take some time to reflect on how it went. Were you able to complete the block as planned? If not, what got in your way? What worked for you this block, and kept you excited for the next workout? And what felt like a drag and kept you from getting out the door? A good training block balances ease with discomfort; the daily work should be easy and enjoyable, and quality sessions should be challenging enough that your body learns to adapt.


### Rework as needed
**When the plan isn’t working for you**, take a moment to reflect on where you’re at, and log it to fitdocs. You can have your LLM create a plan amendment to take an unexpected rest day, move your strength session earlier in the week, swap out a workout for crosstraining, or update the specifics of a workout. Some flexibility is essential when training, especially for athletes balancing a fitness routine on top of family, friends, and work obligations. Too many alterations to a plan is good signal that the plan wasn’t a good fit for one reason or another. Work out why: were you unable to execute the plan due to unrealistic expectations, unforeseen interruptions, or injury? Take what you learn into the next block. If it still isn’t working, you can end it early and begin a more sustainable plan.
